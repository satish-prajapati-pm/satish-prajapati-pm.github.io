01

LumezAI

Concept to production, and still running it after launch.

Domain
AI platform
Role
Sole owner, concept to live operations
Scope
Requirements, roadmap, build coordination, QA, release, operations

An AI product with no delivery structure around it. Someone had to own everything from shaping the requirements to keeping it stable once real users showed up. I took it from concept to a live platform and I run it today.

Decision record

One owner across concept, build and operations means competing priorities every day. How do we get to launch?

  1. Build wide, launch once
  2. Narrow the scope, launch, then iterate on real usage
  3. Hold the launch until it's feature-complete

Why: assumptions about how people use AI products rarely survive production. Live signal beats another planning cycle.

Lesson

Owning a product after launch changes how you plan before it. Logging, failure modes and support paths belong in the roadmap, not the retrospective.

02

Shipped

The House of Rare

Rebuilding a fashion brand's storefront for speed without giving up Shopify.

Client
Rare Rabbit
Domain
Headless commerce
Team
UX, frontend, backend, QA and vendors
Stack
Shopify, Next.js, Unicommerce, Fastrr, Contentsquare, FealtyX

A fast-growing fashion brand had outgrown its Shopify theme. Page speed and the customer experience were both capped by it. I owned the project end to end: estimates, sprint planning, four third-party integrations, QA, release and client communication. We shipped a Next.js storefront on top of Shopify with fulfilment, checkout, loyalty and analytics running in production.

Decision record

Performance and flexibility are capped by the theme layer. What do we change?

  1. Optimise the existing theme
  2. Headless Next.js storefront on top of Shopify
  3. Replace the commerce platform entirely

Why: full control over the frontend and its performance, with no disruption to the catalogue, admin and operations the business already relied on.

Lesson

In a headless build the frontend is the easy part. The real risk sits in the third-party contracts, so integration owners, test data and fallbacks get planned in sprint one, not at launch.

03

Phase 3 in build

My Easy Monogram

From a single-platform Shopify app to a product that doesn't need a store at all.

Domain
Commerce SaaS
Role
Delivery lead, all three phases
Platforms
Shopify, WooCommerce, Etsy
  1. Phase 1Shopify app for one platform's merchants
  2. Phase 2Standalone app for Shopify, WooCommerce and Etsy
  3. Phase 3, nowBuilt-in order management, so merchants can sell with no store

Decision record

Growth is capped by the host platform, and every new channel means rebuilding the same product. How do we expand?

  1. Stay a Shopify app
  2. Build a separate app per platform
  3. One standalone core with a thin integration layer per platform

Why: one product to build and support, three markets to sell into, and a foundation that could later serve merchants with no store at all.

Lesson

Going platform-independent is a product decision long before it's an engineering one. Get the core domain right and every new channel becomes an integration, not a rewrite.

How I work

I turn ambiguity into execution.

  1. 01

    Understand

    Business goals, user needs, constraints and what success looks like, before anything gets planned.

  2. 02

    Structure

    Scope, priorities, roadmap, people and dependencies, in a shape the team can actually execute.

  3. 03

    Align

    Business, design, engineering, QA and stakeholders all describing the plan the same way.

  4. 04

    Execute

    Sprints, tracking and risk management. Momentum comes from decisions made on time.

  5. 05

    Launch

    QA, release planning, deployment and sign-off. A release is a coordinated event.

  6. 06

    Improve

    Measure, learn, iterate. Shipping is where a product starts earning its keep.

What I hold myself to

  • Clarity over complexity. If the team can't explain the goal, the plan isn't ready.
  • Surface risks early. Bad news doesn't get better with age.
  • Context, not status reports. Communication should help people decide.
  • Value, not just deadlines. A product succeeds when it creates value, not when it ships.
  • Fewer meetings, better decisions. Teams need answers, not more calendar invites.

About

I work in the gap between a business goal and a shipped product.

That's where requirements are still vague, three teams disagree, and someone has to make the call.

My approach is simple. Structure the work, make dependencies and risks visible early, keep stakeholders informed instead of stuck in status meetings, and hold the team to outcomes rather than output. Whether it's a 42-person team or a solo AI launch, the job is the same: remove ambiguity so people can do their best work.

I enjoy the tangled problems most. Systems that have to talk to each other, vendors on different timelines, a legacy platform that can't be switched off, a launch date that's already been announced. My computer engineering background means I can follow the trade-offs in a technical discussion, which is usually where delivery risk actually lives.
